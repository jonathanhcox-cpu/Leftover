How much baseboard do you need for a 12×12 room?

By Leftover · Updated September 9, 2026

A rectangular 12 ft × 12 ft room has a 48 linear foot perimeter before subtracting door openings. The amount you buy depends on openings, waste and the stock lengths available.

Calculate my room →
THE MATH

Start with 48 linear feet, then adjust

Perimeter = 2 × (12 + 12) = 48 ft. If one 3-foot doorway interrupts the baseboard, the net run becomes 45 ft. A 10% planning allowance brings that example to 49.5 ft before rounding to full stock pieces.

That does not mean every 12×12 room should buy exactly the same number of boards. Wide openings, closets, built-ins, outside corners and the lengths sold by your supplier change the takeoff. Use the baseboard calculator for your actual dimensions.

REUSE FIRST

Count leftover trim before buying full lengths

If you already have matching baseboard, measure each usable piece. Total footage is only a first check: one six-foot wall run still needs a piece or joint strategy that can span that run. The leftover trim planner can compare individual cuts with full lengths and offcuts.

FROM FOOTAGE TO A SHOPPING LIST

A worked takeoff with one doorway

This example assumes four straight 12-foot walls and one centered 3-foot interruption in the baseboard run. Measure the actual span between the outside edges of the door casing; a door slab advertised as 3 feet wide does not necessarily remove exactly 3 feet of baseboard. Closets and additional wall returns are excluded here.

Room perimeter2 × (12 + 12)48 linear feet

Or round to 12-foot stockRound 49.5 ÷ 12 up5 boards = 60 feet

The reserve is an example planning choice, not a universal requirement. These are purchasing totals before an actual cut plan. Seven 8-foot boards contain enough total length, but a 12-foot wall cannot be covered by one 8-foot piece. That option needs planned joints or different stock. Longer pieces can reduce joints even when their rounded purchasing total is higher.

List each run, not just the total

With the opening centered on one wall, the idealized run list is 12, 12, 12, 4.5 and 4.5 feet. Three 12-foot stock pieces cover the three uninterrupted walls; a fourth can supply the two 4.5-foot sections, leaving room for kerf in its nominal 3-foot remainder. A fifth provides the reserve in the purchasing example above.

Real corners, coping, miters, damaged ends and stock that is not perfectly square can change those cuts. Nominal 12-foot stock may not provide enough excess for a true finished 12-foot run after end trimming. Confirm actual measurements and usable board lengths before buying or cutting.

How do existing offcuts change the order?

Suppose two matching offcuts each have more than 4.5 feet of usable length after cut allowances. They may cover the two short runs, leaving three 12-foot runs to buy. Do not subtract an offcut simply because its label says “4.5 ft”: it must still fit the finished cut after damaged ends and joint details are allowed for.

Use the individual-piece trim planner for that fit check. Keep baseboard, shoe molding and door casing in separate lists; they are different profiles and often have different run lengths.

What else should I measure?

  • Every wall at floor level, including recesses and returns.
  • Actual gaps where baseboard stops, such as door casing or built-in cabinets.
  • Inside and outside corners and the intended joint method.
  • Closets separately, if they are part of the job.

A quick sketch and a labeled run list are more useful than increasing the waste percentage to compensate for missing measurements.
